You're not being ridiculous, and you're not an asshole. You're having a very normal "I'm loyal to people, not logos" reaction.

Two things can be true at the same time:

* **Your boss did right by you** (and it meant something).
* **You can still leave** (and it can be the right move for your life).

Gratitude doesn't create a lifetime contract. It creates an obligation to act with integrity and kindness on the way out.

### Reframe that helps

Try swapping "I'm betraying him" for: **"He helped me grow, and now I'm making a next-step decision."**

A good manager usually *wants* their people to move toward better-fit work, even if it stings. And the things you described—protecting you during layoffs, flexibility during family illness—those are marks of someone who understands the human side of work. That kind of person is also the kind of person who can handle a respectful resignation.

### What makes it feel like betrayal (and how to defuse it)

It feels personal because:

* He advocated for you.
* He supported you during a tough family time.
* You feel like you "owe" him.

The way to honor that isn't to stay out of guilt. It's to:

* **Tell him directly first** (no rumors).
* **Be clear and decisive** (don't make him talk you into staying when your mind is made up).
* **Express specific gratitude** (name the exact things he did).
* **Make the transition easier** (offer a clean handoff).

### A simple script you can use (keep it short)

You can say something like:

> "I wanted to tell you directly before anyone else. I've accepted an offer that's a better fit for where I want to take my career—more aligned work, remote, and it makes sense for my family. This wasn't an easy decision because I'm genuinely grateful for how you've supported me—especially during the layoffs and when my dad was sick. I won't forget that. I'm giving my notice today, and I want to do everything I can to make the transition smooth. I can document everything, train someone up, and help you prioritize what should be handed off."

That covers: respect, clarity, gratitude, and support.

### Expect a reaction (and don't interpret it as "you're wrong")

Even good bosses can have a moment of disappointment or frustration. That doesn't mean you did something wrong. It means they're human and now they have a staffing problem. Let them have their feelings without you taking responsibility for them.

If he says things like:

* **"Is it money?"** → "Comp is part of it, but the bigger thing is role alignment and remote."
* **"Can we match?"** → If you're decided: "I really appreciate it, but I've made my decision."
* **"I'm surprised"** → "I get that. I wanted to tell you face-to-face because of how much I respect you."

### Do this before Friday

Since you've basically decided and there's a deadline, the cleanest path is:

1. **Accept the offer in writing** (and confirm start date, remote terms, any contingencies).
2. **Tell your boss ASAP after it's accepted** (ideally same day).
3. **Give a standard notice** (two weeks unless your situation differs).
4. **Send a short follow-up email** recapping resignation date and transition plan.

(If your boss is the type to react badly, you can still keep it professional and brief. But given what you described, chances are he'll handle it better than your anxiety is predicting.)

### One mindset shift that makes this easier

Instead of thinking: "I'm leaving *him*," think: **"I'm leaving a role, and I'm keeping a relationship."**

If you handle this well, you can absolutely keep him as:

* a mentor,
* a reference,
* someone you grab coffee with later.
