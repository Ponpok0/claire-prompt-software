It is completely normal to feel torn in this situation. The guilt you are experiencing isn't a sign that you are making the wrong decision; it is a sign that you have a high level of integrity and that you value the human side of your professional relationships.

Your wife is correct that employment is a contract, but you are also right: when someone uses their political capital to save your job or supports you through a family crisis, a psychological bond is formed that goes beyond a standard paycheck.

Here is a perspective shift and a plan to handle the conversation with integrity.

### 1. Reframing the "Betrayal"

You are feeling like you are breaking a loyalty pact. However, great managers usually have a different perspective on this than employees do.

* **Mentorship, not Ownership:** A truly good boss (the kind who protects their team) knows that their role is to help their employees grow, even if that growth eventually takes them out of the company.
* **Repayment was your Performance:** You likely "repaid" his support by doing good work, being reliable, and sticking around for the last two years. You didn't owe him your career in perpetuity; you owed him your best effort while you were there.
* **The "Bus Factor":** By leaving on good terms, you aren't betraying him; you are becoming a valuable part of his external network.

### 2. The Conversation Strategy

Since you have a strong relationship, you owe him a conversation that is transparent and centers on gratitude rather than just logistics.

**The Setup:**
Do not do this over email or Slack. Schedule a 1:1 video call or meeting as soon as possible. Do not wait until Friday; giving him as much notice as possible is a sign of respect.

**The Script Structure:**

1. **Lead with the news (Rip the Band-Aid):** Don't bury the lead.
2. **Focus on the "Pull," not the "Push":** Explain that you are running *toward* a specific opportunity (remote work, role alignment), not running *away* from him or the company.
3. **Acknowledge the specific debt:** This is the most important part for clearing your conscience. Explicitly mention the times he helped you.

**Example Talking Points:**

> "I have some difficult news. I've received an offer from another company, and I've decided to accept it.
> This was a genuinely hard decision because of how much I value working with you. I haven't forgotten that you went to bat for me during the layoffs two years ago, or how flexible you were when my dad was sick. That support meant the world to me and my family.
> However, this new role offers full remote work and a path that aligns perfectly with where I want to take my career long-term. I want to do everything I can to make this transition as smooth as possible for you."

### 3. Anticipating the Reaction

Because he is a good boss, his initial reaction might be disappointment, but his secondary reaction will likely be congratulations. If he was willing to protect you during layoffs, he likely wants what is best for you as a person.

If you stay solely out of guilt, you will likely begin to resent the current job as you miss out on the better pay and remote lifestyle. That resentment will eventually degrade the very relationship you are trying to protect.

### Summary

You are not "just doing business"; you are evolving your career. You can honor the past relationship by handling the exit with the same class and care your boss showed you during the hard times.
