There's a premise buried in everything you just said that's worth pulling out before we go anywhere else: the idea that "gifted" was a prediction. It wasn't. It was a description of how your brain processed information relative to other kids at age 8 or 10 or whatever. That's it. Teachers aren't oracles. They saw a kid who learned fast and said "this one's sharp," and somehow that got alchemized into a life sentence of expectation that you've been serving for two decades.

The word "potential" is doing a lot of heavy lifting in your framing, and I think it's worth asking what it actually points to. Potential *for what*? When people told you that you had potential, they never finished the sentence. Potential to become a physicist? A CEO? A novelist? They didn't know. They just meant "this kid seems like raw material for something impressive," and then left you holding an open-ended debt with no terms and no maturity date. That's not a gift. That's an unfunded mandate.

Here's the thing that's structurally off about "accept I'm ordinary and stop chasing a fantasy": you're framing this as though there are two doors, one labeled EXTRAORDINARY and one labeled ORDINARY, and since door one didn't open, you're resigned to door two. But that binary is the actual problem, not the answer to it. The entire framework of "extraordinary vs. ordinary" was installed by the same people who told a child they were destined for greatness without defining what that meant. You're still operating inside their frame, just from the losing side of it now.

What you're describing with "nothing sticks" is also interesting, because it might not be evidence that you lack drive. It might be evidence that you keep picking things based on whether they'd make you look like you fulfilled the prophecy, rather than whether they actually hold your attention for their own sake. There's a real difference between "I tried this because it seemed like what a gifted person should do" and "I tried this because I genuinely couldn't stop thinking about it." If everything you've attempted has been auditioned against the question "is this extraordinary enough?", then of course nothing sticks. You're filtering for the wrong variable.

So here's where I'd push back on your conclusion. "Making peace with being ordinary" still accepts the scoreboard. It's just conceding the game instead of questioning whether the game was worth playing. The more useful question might be: if nobody had ever called you gifted, if that word had never entered your vocabulary, what would you actually want to be doing? Not what would impress anyone, not what would retroactively justify the label, but what would you do if the concept of "wasted potential" simply didn't exist as a category?

Because that's the fork, really. It's not extraordinary vs. ordinary. It's "living against a story someone else wrote" vs. "figuring out what you actually care about without the ghost of a ten-year-old's test scores watching over your shoulder."
