This is a really common and genuinely painful experience, and I want to push back gently on the framing before helping you make peace with anything.

The "gifted kid" label often does more harm than good. What it usually means is that you learned certain academic skills faster than your peers *as a child*. That's it. But the adults around you turned that into a prophecy — "you'll do something extraordinary" — without giving you a roadmap, and often without teaching you the things that actually matter for long-term fulfillment: how to tolerate being bad at something new, how to persist when motivation fades, how to choose a direction when you could go several ways. So you ended up with high expectations and, very likely, a deep habit of abandoning things when they stop feeling effortless. That's not a character flaw. It's a predictable outcome of how you were raised.

A few things worth sitting with:

**The "nothing sticks" pattern might be the actual problem, not evidence of ordinariness.** You mention side projects, courses, going back to school — all abandoned. That's worth being curious about rather than resigned about. What happens right before you quit? Is it boredom, fear of not being great at it, loss of novelty? Because if you're dropping things at the first plateau, you're not discovering that you're ordinary — you're just never getting past the part where every single person, gifted or not, has to grind.

**"Extraordinary" is a terrible goal.** It's not actionable. You can't wake up and work toward extraordinary. You can wake up and work toward something specific that matters to you. The gifted kid trap is wanting to *be* something rather than wanting to *do* something. What have you actually enjoyed doing, even briefly, before you dropped it?

**Your job isn't your worth, and "average" isn't a verdict.** Plenty of people doing meaningful, interesting things have unremarkable day jobs. The question isn't whether your job title impresses people. It's whether your life feels like yours.

So here's what I'd actually suggest instead of "making peace with being ordinary": get curious about why nothing sticks. Maybe work with a therapist who understands gifted kid burnout — it's a well-recognized pattern. And try picking one thing, something you find genuinely interesting, not something that sounds impressive, and commit to staying with it past the point where it stops being easy. Six months minimum. See what happens.

You might find that the problem was never your potential. It was that nobody taught you what to do with it.
