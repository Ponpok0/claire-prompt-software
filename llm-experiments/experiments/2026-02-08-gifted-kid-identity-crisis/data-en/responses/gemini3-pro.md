This is a heavy burden to carry, and I want to start by validating exactly where you are. What you are describing is often called "Gifted Kid Burnout," and it is a painful, confusing place to be.

The label of "gifted" is often less of a compliment and more of a **prophecy** that children are expected to fulfill. When that prophecy doesn't materialize in the way the world expects (Nobel prizes, CEO titles, fame), it feels like a moral failing.

Making peace with being "ordinary" isn't about giving up; it is about **liberating yourself from a contract you never signed.**

Here is a framework for navigating this transition from "failed potential" to a peaceful reality.

---

### 1. Grieve the Fantasy Version of Yourself

The "extraordinary" version of you that you've been chasing isn't real, but your attachment to it is. To move forward, you have to grieve that imaginary person as if they were a loved one who passed away.

* **The Loss:** You are mourning the loss of the future you were promised. It is okay to feel sad, angry, or embarrassed about this.
* **The Reality:** That fantasy version of you was likely fueled by anxiety and external pressure, not your own genuine desires. Letting them go is an act of mercy toward your current self.

### 2. Understand the "Fixed Mindset" Trap

"Gifted" children are often praised for their *innate intelligence* rather than their *effort*. This creates a psychological trap:

* If success comes from being smart, then having to try hard means you aren't smart.
* Therefore, if a new skill (like the courses you mentioned) gets difficult, you abandon it because the struggle threatens your identity.
* **The Shift:** You stop trying to be "good" at things immediately. You give yourself permission to be a beginner, to be slow, and to be bad at things.

### 3. Redefine "Ordinary"

Society tells us that "average" is an insult. In reality, a statistically average life is actually a **sustainable** life.

* **The IT Support Role:** You mentioned working in IT support. While it may not feel glamorous, it is foundational. You solve problems, you help people function, and you likely have stability. That is not "wasting" potential; that is contributing to the functioning of society.
* **The Freedom of Anonymity:** When you stop trying to be extraordinary, you no longer have to perform for an audience. You can just *exist*. You can watch a movie without analyzing it, take a walk without tracking your steps, or learn to cook without starting a food blog.

### 4. Separate Your "Self" from Your "Output"

The "gifted" label ties your worth as a human being to your economic or academic output.

* **The Truth:** You are 34. You are a complete person regardless of your job title. Your value is found in how you treat your friends, how you take care of your health, how you enjoy a Sunday morning, and how you show up for the people you love.
* **The Goal:** Shift your focus from *achievement* to *connection* and *contentment*.

### 5. Cultivate "Low-Stakes" Hobbies

You mentioned that side projects don't stick. That is likely because you are subconsciously turning them into new jobs or tests of your potential.

* **The Antidote:** Pick a hobby that you are terrible at and have no intention of monetizing or mastering.
* **Examples:** Bad watercolor painting, slow jogging, reading "trashy" novels.
* **Why:** Doing something purely for the joy of the *process*, with zero care for the *result*, retrains your brain to enjoy life without the pressure of "potential."

---

### The Reality Check

At 34, you are young enough to change your life, but you are old enough to realize that **happiness is rarely found in the places we were told to look for it.**

Accepting that you are "ordinary" is not a defeat. It is the moment you stop hyperventilating and finally take a full, deep breath. It is the moment you stop living for a hypothetical future and start inhabiting your actual life.
