Your frustration makes complete sense — putting in that level of effort with so little return is genuinely demoralizing, and watching someone seemingly coast into a position through connections can feel like a gut punch.

But I'd push back a little on the framing. It's not that hard work is a lie — it's that **where** you direct that effort matters enormously, and the job market has some uncomfortable realities worth facing honestly.

**What's actually going on:** Studies consistently show that a huge percentage of jobs (estimates vary, but often cited around 70%+) are filled through some form of networking or internal referral. That doesn't mean applications are useless, but it does mean that treating applications as your *primary* strategy is a bit like playing on hard mode by choice. Your friend's experience isn't a fluke — it's actually closer to how hiring typically works.

**That said, "give up on applications entirely" is the wrong takeaway.** Here's why:

Applications still work for certain roles and companies, especially larger organizations with structured hiring pipelines. And your 5 interviews from 200 applications (~2.5% hit rate) is actually not terrible in this market — the conversion problem might be at the interview stage, not the application stage. That's worth examining separately.

**What I'd actually suggest is a rebalance, not an abandonment.** Something like shifting from 90% applications / 10% networking to maybe 40/60 the other direction. Concretely that could look like reaching out to people at companies you're interested in for informational conversations (not "can you get me a job" but genuine curiosity about their work), attending industry events or online communities, reconnecting with former colleagues, and yes — still applying, but more selectively and ideally to places where you've made some kind of human contact first.

A few other things worth reflecting on: if you've had 5 interviews and no offers, there may be something to tune in how you're interviewing — practicing with someone who can give you honest feedback could be more valuable than sending out another 50 applications. And 200 tailored applications in two months is a pace that might be leading to burnout, which can subtly show up in your energy during interviews.

You're clearly not lazy — the issue isn't effort, it's allocation. Your friend's approach isn't "not trying hard," it's trying differently. You can do both.
