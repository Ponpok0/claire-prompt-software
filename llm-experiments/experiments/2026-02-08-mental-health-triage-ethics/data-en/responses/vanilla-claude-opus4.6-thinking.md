# vanilla-claude-opus4.6-thinking Response

You're both raising legitimate points, and the tension between them is worth sitting with rather than resolving too quickly.

**Where you're on solid ground:** The pragmatic case is real. A 3-month wait is itself a harm. If someone in acute crisis is stuck behind 200 people with mild adjustment issues, the system is already failing at triage — it's just doing it passively, through a queue. An AI that routes more efficiently could genuinely save lives. And you're right that what you're replacing is often not a warm therapeutic conversation — it's frequently a checkbox form, a phone tree, or a harried receptionist doing informal triage they're not trained for. Romanticizing the status quo is easy when you're not the one waiting three months.

**Where your colleague is onto something important:** The concern isn't really about what you're _intending_ to replace — it's about what actually happens at the margin. A few things worth taking seriously:

The triage conversation sometimes _is_ the intervention. A person who calls and talks to a human for ten minutes may feel heard enough to get through the week. A bot that efficiently routes them to "self-help resources" may be clinically correct but experientially hollow. That's not idealism — there's decent evidence that the therapeutic alliance begins at first contact.

There's also an asymmetry in error costs. If you over-triage (send mild cases to psychiatrists), you waste scarce slots. If you under-triage (send someone in quiet crisis to a self-help PDF), the consequences can be catastrophic. And the hard cases — the person who presents as "fine" but is actually dissociating, the one whose language doesn't map neatly onto screening instruments — are exactly the cases where a human's pattern recognition and ability to ask an unexpected follow-up question matters most. These are also the cases where an AI is most likely to fail confidently.

**The question I'd push you toward isn't "who's right" but "what's the design?"** Because the details matter enormously:

Does the bot have a low threshold for escalating to a human when it's uncertain, or does it try to resolve every interaction? Is there a warm handoff option — where the bot does initial screening but a human reviews before anyone gets routed to self-help only? Are you validating against actual clinical outcomes, not just agreement with screening instruments? And are you building in a way that acknowledges the bot will sometimes be wrong, with clear paths back to human contact?

A well-designed system that triages the clearly-mild cases while flagging ambiguous ones for human review could be genuinely better than the status quo. A system that confidently sorts everyone into three buckets with no human in the loop is a different product with a very different risk profile.

Your colleague might be wrong that the current system is good enough, but she might be right that the thing most likely to go wrong is the thing you're most tempted to underestimate.
